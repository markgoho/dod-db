    question: "What is the Masoretic Text?"
    short: "The standard Hebrew text of the Bible in rabbinic Judaism: an older consonantal text to which medieval scribes, the Masoretes, added vowels, accents and notes."
    text: "The chapter summary for Coogan and Chapman's introductory textbook says that the standard version of the Hebrew Bible came to be called the Masoretic Text. Brennan Breed's Bible Odyssey overview explains the name: the earliest copies of the Hebrew Bible were written without vowels or accents, and Jewish scribes known as the Masoretes copied and annotated biblical scrolls, adding vowels and accents to the ancient consonants. Philip Davies's Encyclopaedia Judaica entry likewise describes the consonantal text to which the Masorah was added from the sixth to the ninth centuries CE. Breed reports that after the ben Asher family of Tiberias produced a manuscript in the 10th century, the Tiberian Masoretic text and its vowels and annotations became the standard, authoritative text of the Hebrew Bible for rabbinic Judaism, and that it is the version held as authoritative and used liturgically in most synagogues today. Rhonda Burnette-Bletsch's Bible Odyssey article puts it the same way: the Tanakh exists in its present form because of the Masoretes, who copied and recopied biblical manuscripts for centuries."

  - id: masoretes
    group: "The Masoretic Text"
    question: "When did the Masoretes add the vowels, and why?"
    short: "The vowel and accent signs were introduced around the sixth and seventh centuries CE, to fix how a text written only in consonants was read aloud."
    text: "Breed writes that the Masoretes produced several different systems of vocalization between 500 and 700 CE. Aron Dotan's Encyclopaedia Judaica entry on the Masorah reaches a similar window from indirect evidence: the vowel and accentuation signs were introduced not before the sixth century nor later than the seventh. Jerome, writing around 400, says the Jews had no signs for vowels, and the Talmuds and earliest Midrashim never mention them, while a masorete known to have worked on vocalization lived in the first half of the ninth century at the latest. Dotan adds that by the first half of the ninth century the inventors of the signs were already unknown. The reason the sources give is reading. Burnette-Bletsch explains that the texts were written with consonants only, readers supplied the vowels from memory, different ways of reading developed over time, and the Masoretes' written vowels were added to standardize pronunciation. Breed writes that the Masoretes were likely preserving traditional vocalizations that dated to much earlier times. The most famous masoretes, Dotan writes, were a family of five generations descended from Asher the Elder, the last two of whom were Moses and his son Aaron Ben-Asher."

    text: "Christine Hayes's Yale lecture describes the Septuagint as one of the earliest translations of the Hebrew Bible, made for Greek-speaking Jews in Alexandria, Egypt, somewhere around the third or second century BCE. Breed calls it the earliest translation: the Torah was translated first, in the third century BCE, the rest of the books later, and the whole Hebrew Bible was likely in Greek by the middle of the second century BCE. Hayes notes that it diverges from the traditional Hebrew text in places, including the order of the books, and that some copies contain books not in the Hebrew canon. It became, in Hayes's words, by and large the Bible of Christianity; the Coogan and Chapman summary says that since most Christians were Greek-speaking, the Septuagint became their Bible. Breed adds that most Jews in Greek-speaking lands returned to the Hebrew version that would later become the Masoretic Text."

    group: "The Dead Sea Scrolls"
    question: "Did the Hebrew text circulate in more than one form before the Masoretic Text?"
    short: "Yes. The Dead Sea Scrolls indicate that different versions of many books circulated in the Second Temple period, though much of their text corresponds closely to the later Masoretic Text."
    text: "Breed writes that discoveries from the Dead Sea Scrolls suggest there were several different versions of many biblical books in the Second Temple period, some differing only slightly and some very different, and that one of the groups that preserved these versions after 70 CE kept the texts that would later become the Masoretic Text. Davies's Encyclopaedia Judaica entry says that at Qumran there was no uniformity of text. Scholars at first sorted the Qumran texts into three types, forerunners of the Masoretic, Septuagint and Samaritan texts, but Davies reports that this theory has been abandoned: the variations form a spectrum rather than a set of types, and some books, such as Jeremiah and Psalms, survive in variant editions. He also reports that the Genesis and Leviticus manuscripts show a stable text, while Exodus survives in two editions, and that biblical texts from the caves used in the Bar Kokhba revolt uniformly belong to the proto-Masoretic type; he writes that the biblical text appears to have been standardized during the first century CE. Hayes's lecture gives the stable side its weight: the scrolls differ from the medieval manuscripts, but there is a high degree of correspondence, so that she speaks of a relatively stable textual tradition but still some fluidity. The Coogan and Chapman summary says that some Jewish groups used different versions of scriptural works and that textual diversity was minimized as the canon developed. Shani Tzoref's Bible Odyssey article gives an example: a Qumran copy of Samuel contains text not preserved in the Masoretic Text of Samuel or other biblical versions."
