Christine Hayes's Yale lecture likewise finds two flood stories in Genesis 6–9 with distinctive styles, themes, vocabularies and details, interwoven rather than placed side by side as the two creation stories are, and the chapter summary for Coogan and Chapman's textbook says that in Genesis 6.5–9.17 the J and P sources, which until then appear separately, are interconnected and woven together. The sources give their reasons. The story tells things twice and the tellings disagree: Noah is told to bring two of every kind of animal in one place and seven pairs of clean animals and one pair of unclean in another; the flood lasts 40 days in one verse and 150 in another; and some passages name God Yahweh (“the LORD”) and others Elohim (“God”). Geoghegan notes that earlier interpreters reconciled these differences one by one, as phases of the flood or as an expanded command, and allows that this may be; it is their number and character, here and in many other duplicate stories, that persuades most scholars. On this reading the seven pairs of clean animals belong to the tradition in which Noah offers a sacrifice after the flood, and the other tradition, often associated with priestly interests, has no such sacrifice, since sacrifice is the domain of priests. The New Catholic Encyclopedia adds that when the J elements are taken out, the P parts form a complete and consistent narrative."

  - id: geology
    group: "Was there a flood?"
    question: "Does geology support a worldwide flood?"
    short: "No. Geologists dismissed a global flood in the 19th century, and no geological evidence suggests one."
    text: "The geologist David Montgomery, writing in GSA Today, recounts how geologists came to dismiss the idea of a global flood. William Buckland, Oxford's first geology professor, had gathered evidence for one in 1823, but by 1836 acknowledged that there was no geological evidence of a global flood after all; Louis Agassiz argued to geologists that most of the evidence once read as a global flood recorded a flood of ice; and Charles Lyell argued that the fragile cinder cones of southern France could not have survived a flood strong enough to carve the valleys cut into the lava flows that came from them. By the end of the nineteenth century, Montgomery writes, conservative Christians generally accepted that there was no geological support for reading Noah's flood as a globe-wrecking deluge. Belief in a global flood was revived by John Whitcomb and Henry Morris's The Genesis Flood (1961), whose authors had no geological training. Montgomery concludes that the ideas invoked to explain such a flood have been refuted time and again and that there is no geologic evidence of a global deluge. The New Catholic Encyclopedia entry says that no scientific, geological or historical evidence suggests that a single flood ever wiped out all civilisation in Mesopotamia, let alone the whole world, and that marine fossils found in mountains are the result of geological uplift."

  - id: black-sea
    group: "Was there a flood?"
    question: "Was it the flooding of the Black Sea?"
    short: "A hypothesis first put forward in 1997 says so; two later overviews of the evidence find that the Black Sea's reconnection with the Mediterranean was gradual, not a catastrophic flood."
    text: "V. Yanko-Hombach and her co-authors describe the hypothesis, first put forward by Ryan and colleagues in 1997 and modified by them in 2003: an abrupt and catastrophic inflow of Mediterranean water raised the level of the Black Sea, and the resulting inundation and environmental change shaped the biblical flood legend. Their review of the geological, palaeontological and archaeological record finds that the Black Sea rose only gradually when it reconnected with the Sea of Marmara, that the evidence does not sustain any catastrophic flood in the early Holocene, and that there was no horde of refugees carrying a flood legend south to Mesopotamia; barring new evidence, they conclude that the Early Holocene Black Sea flood is a contemporary legend. A. E. Aksu and R. N. Hiscott's 2022 review of the evidence from 1997 to 2021 likewise finds that the reconnection of the Black Sea with the Mediterranean was gradual and that their observations are incompatible with that reconnection having been a catastrophic flood."
