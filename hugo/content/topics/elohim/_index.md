The New English Translation renders Elohim as judges in Exodus 21 and 22, and its footnote cites two Journal of Biblical Literature articles as support. Both articles, Cyrus Gordon's from 1935 and Anne Draffkorn's from 1957, conclude that the word means gods, not judges. The judges reading is old: Targum Onkelos, the Peshitta, Rashi, and the King James Version all take it, because a plural verb next to Elohim makes the plain reading a court that brings people before the gods. Genesis 20:13 has the same problem, where Abraham says Elohim caused him to wander with a plural verb, and 1 Samuel 28, where the necromancer of Endor sees Elohim rising with a plural participle and Saul asks what he looks like.

The grammar also undercuts two popular readings. The Shema calls Adonai Elohenu, your God, which uses the word with a possessive suffix to pick out the one god among many that belongs to Israel, not to deny that the others exist. And the plural form has nothing to do with the Trinity: Elohim behaves like a concretized abstract plural that takes singular or plural agreement as context requires. The word was also distinctive enough that biblical writers mocked other gods by bending its sound, calling them elilim and gilulim, the second probably derived from a word for dung.
