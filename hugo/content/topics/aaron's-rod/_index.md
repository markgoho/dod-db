Three items reportedly sat before the Ark of the Covenant: the tablets of the law, a jar of manna, and Aaron's rod — a staff that, according to Numbers 17, sprouted buds, blossoms, and ripe almonds overnight to settle a dispute over which tribe held priestly authority. Raanan Eichler's argument that the budding-staff story was invented later to explain away an already-existing Asherah pole kept beside the Ark turns a minor tribal miracle into a test case for whether official Israelite worship was quietly making room for a goddess.

The same staff appears again two chapters later in Numbers 20, where Moses takes the staff from before the LORD — not his own staff, but Aaron's — to strike the rock at Meribah, the single act that costs him entry into the promised land. Read as an Asherah pole rather than a plain walking stick, that punishment looks sharper: Moses is barred from Canaan for misusing a divine object, while Aaron, who built and led worship of an actual golden calf earlier in the same book, faces no consequence at all. Guest scholar Angela Roskop Erisman reads the same image differently: for her, a flowering staff evoking Isaiah's shoot from the stump of Jesse signals that the Aaron narratives are doing deliberate royal-messianic work, not just settling a dispute over priestly turf.
