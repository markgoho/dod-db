Ugaritic was deciphered from thirty cuneiform signs found on tablets a French farmer's plow turned up near the Syrian coast in 1929, and among those tablets is a myth cycle following the storm god Baal — a second-tier deity, not the head of the pantheon — through three linked story arcs: a fight against the sea god Yam for kingship, a campaign to win a palace, and a battle against the death god Mot. The pantheon's presiding figure, El, is elderly and benevolent, exactly the profile of the god later fused with a distinct, younger warrior-storm deity called Adonai in early Israelite religion; line the Baal Cycle up against that fusion and the split holds up piece by piece, with El matching the patriarch who cedes practical rule and Baal matching the youthful figure who rides storms into battle.

The overlap extends past character types into vocabulary the Hebrew Bible reuses directly: Baal is hailed as "Charioteer of the Clouds," a title the Bible later gives Adonai, and Baal's fight against the sea monster Lotan supplies the cognate name behind Leviathan, the chaos serpent that shows up in Isaiah, Job, and the Psalms. Because the Ugaritic texts predate the Hebrew Bible's composition by five or six centuries and the city of Ugarit was destroyed before Israel appears in the historical record, no biblical author could have read them directly — the shared language and imagery point instead to a common Northwest Semitic literary inheritance that Israelite and Judahite scribes drew on independently.
