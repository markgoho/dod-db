Second Kings describes Josiah's men hauling an Asherah pole out of the Jerusalem temple, grinding it to dust, and burning the remains in the Kidron Valley — a scene specific enough that most historians treat it as evidence the pole was actually there, whatever they make of the sweeping reform narrative built around it. What the object actually was is less settled: probably a wooden pole or stylized tree standing in for the goddess Asherah, echoed in the stylized date palms and feeding ibexes carved on cult stands and painted on pottery at sites like Kuntillet Ajrud.

Before Josiah's reforms made the pole disreputable, the biblical text treats Asherah's presence beside Adonai as unremarkable: the evidence suggests she was worshiped as his consort rather than a rival to be purged until the seventh century BCE. Raanan Eichler pushes past that acceptance: he argues the story of Aaron's flowering rod, kept beside the Ark of the Covenant, was invented specifically to explain away an actual Asherah pole sitting in the Holy of Holies, folding a once-ordinary cult object into an origin story about priestly authority instead.
